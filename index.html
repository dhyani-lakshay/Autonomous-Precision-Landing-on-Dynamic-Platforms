Landing a quadcopter on a moving rover

A flight controller that finds a rover by its ArUco marker, matches its speed, and sets down on it at 240 Hz. It works in calm air and in random wind.

The landing, phase by phase

The drone starts 3 m up with the rover right below it, already driving away at about 1.7 m/s on a curving path. The controller never gets the rover's position from the simulator; everything it knows about the rover comes through the camera.

  1. SearchFind the marker. The first clean detection locks its ID.
  2. TrackHold 3 m and match the rover's motion. Move on after 0.6 s within 12 cm and 0.25 m/s of it.
  3. DescendSink at 0.9, then 0.6, then 0.4 m/s. Slow down or stop if alignment slips; climb back to Track if the rover is lost.
  4. FinalBelow about 0.4 m the camera can't see the deck. Sink at 0.35 m/s on the Kalman prediction alone.
  5. Cut-offOn contact, and within 35 cm of the centre, set all four motors to zero.

Seeing the marker all the way down

From high up, OpenCV's ArUco detector finds the whole marker. Every image point is projected onto the deck by intersecting its camera ray with the deck plane, using the drone's exact pose:

\[ \mathbf{r} = R\begin{bmatrix} -\tfrac{v-c_y}{f} \\ -\tfrac{u-c_x}{f} \\ -1 \end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{P} = \mathbf{C} + \frac{z_{deck}-C_z}{r_z}\,\mathbf{r},\qquad f = \frac{120}{\tan 30^\circ} \approx 207.8\ \text{px} \]

The camera's 60° field of view is vertical, so it sees a strip of ground about 1.15 times its height. Below roughly 1 m, the 0.8 m marker no longer fits and ArUco fails for every ID.

That is where template matching takes over. Having read the ID once, the controller redraws the exact black-and-white pattern, renders what the camera should see at the predicted rover position, and slides the real image over it with normalised cross-correlation. The best match gives the correction. It stays accurate until the camera is 17 cm from the deck.

Checked on all 100 marker IDs: 1,200 low-altitude views, worst error 4.5 mm.

Tracking the rover

Each accepted measurement feeds a six-state Kalman filter that assumes roughly constant acceleration. It estimates velocity and acceleration from position alone, and keeps predicting through the final blind stretch.

\[ \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x & y & v_x & v_y & a_x & a_y \end{bmatrix}^{T},\qquad \begin{bmatrix} p\\ v\\ a \end{bmatrix}_{k+1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \Delta t & \tfrac12\Delta t^2 \\ 0 & 1 & \Delta t \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} p\\ v\\ a \end{bmatrix}_{k} \]

A measurement is only used if it is plausible given the estimate. The Mahalanobis distance must be under 40 for ArUco and under 25 for template matching:

\[ d^{2} = (\mathbf{z}-H\mathbf{x})^{T}\left(HPH^{T}+R_m\right)^{-1}(\mathbf{z}-H\mathbf{x}) \]

From error to motor forces

The drone copies the rover's estimated acceleration, adds corrections for position and velocity error, and subtracts the estimated wind:

\[ \mathbf{a}_{xy} = \mathbf{a}_{rover} + K_p\,\mathbf{e}_p + K_d\,\mathbf{e}_v - \frac{\mathbf{F}_{wind,xy}}{m},\qquad a_z = 16\,(z_{ref}-z) + 8\,(v_{z,ref}-v_z) - \frac{F_{wind,z}}{m} \]

The wanted force sets the thrust direction. A geometric attitude controller tilts the drone towards it, keeping the current heading:

\[ \mathbf{F}_{des} = m(\mathbf{a}_{des} + g\hat{\mathbf{z}}),\qquad \mathbf{e}_R = \tfrac12\,\mathrm{vee}\!\left(R_{des}^{T}R - R^{T}R_{des}\right),\qquad \boldsymbol{\tau} = -I\omega_n^{2}\,\mathbf{e}_R - 2\zeta\omega_n I\,\boldsymbol{\omega} \]

Here \(I = 0.018\ \text{kg·m}^2\), \(\omega_n = 14\) rad/s and \(\zeta = 0.9\). Total thrust is the part of the wanted force along the drone's current up-axis. It is split across the four motors, which sit at \(\pm L\) with \(L = 0.25\) m:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_1 &= \tfrac{T}{4} + \tfrac{\tau_x}{4L} - \tfrac{\tau_y}{4L}, & f_2 &= \tfrac{T}{4} + \tfrac{\tau_x}{4L} + \tfrac{\tau_y}{4L}, \\ f_3 &= \tfrac{T}{4} - \tfrac{\tau_x}{4L} - \tfrac{\tau_y}{4L}, & f_4 &= \tfrac{T}{4} - \tfrac{\tau_x}{4L} + \tfrac{\tau_y}{4L} \end{aligned} \]

Wind

PyBullet updates velocity exactly, and the controller knows its own thrust and gravity. Whatever is left over is an outside force:

\[ \mathbf{F}_{ext} = m\,\frac{\Delta\mathbf{v}}{\Delta t} - \mathbf{F}_{thrust} - \mathbf{F}_{gravity} \]

The estimate is smoothed over about 50 ms and always subtracted in the control law. In calm air it stays near zero, so one controller serves both arenas. When its 1-second average passes 0.30 N, wind mode adds 8° of tilt allowance; it switches off again below 0.12 N.

Run it yourself

You need Python 3.8 or newer and OpenCV 4.7 or newer with the ArUco module. The arenas call generateImageMarker, which older versions lack. Tested with Python 3.12, PyBullet 3.2.7, NumPy 2.4 and OpenCV 4.13.

pip uninstall -y opencv-python opencv-python-headless opencv-contrib-python
pip install pybullet numpy "opencv-contrib-python>=4.7"

python local_arena.py          # calm air
python local_areana_wind.py    # random wind gusts

To try other marker IDs without editing the arenas:

python test_all_markers.py 5 42 99      # selected IDs
python test_all_markers.py --both       # IDs 0–99, with and without wind

Full setup notes and troubleshooting are in the README.

Limitations

Everything here runs in simulation, where the drone's pose is known exactly and there is no motor lag or drag. The last stretch of the descent relies on prediction, so a long loss of the marker lets the position error grow. A physical drone would need real state estimation and more sensors before any of this carries over.
